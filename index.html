Models only recently got good enough at execution that deciding became the constraint. That is the part we are building.

A general model brought to a specialist decision has no priors but the internet’s.

Judgment is not reasoning in a vacuum. It is a field’s accumulated frameworks, the things it has already established, the methods that actually measure it, and a record of what has worked and what hasn’t. Take those away and a very capable model will reason fluently to an average answer — and sound certain doing it.

That body of work doesn’t exist for marketing, and it doesn’t exist for most fields. As long as a person owns the decision, the reasoning behind it rarely leaves their head. Nothing is written down, so nothing is scored, so the next decision starts where the last one did.

Nothing compounds.

We are building a judgment engine.

One half assembles what a decision rests on. The other is the research into what should be making the decision.

The context layer

Everything a decision in the field rests on, gathered in one place. Today it is scattered across tools, or held in someone’s head.

Frameworks
What the field has already established, loaded as priors — and revised when the evidence disagrees.
Past learnings
What was decided and why, what it weighed, what was predicted, and what actually happened.
Performance data
Every platform the work runs on, reconciled to one grain instead of one dashboard each.
Category context
Where demand is, who else is competing for it, and which way the market is moving.
Company context
Goals, constraints and economics — margin, inventory, cash, and what this particular effort is for.
Retrieval and scoping
Which slice of the record bears on this question, how far to look, and when the read is too thin to answer at all. Current technique gets a useful version; choosing what to look at before you know what is wrong is still open.

Built properly, this pays before any of the research lands: point a frontier model at it and it already does better than the same model working from what is scattered across tools today. That is the floor rather than the goal — the step after is replacing that general reasoner with methods built for this kind of decision.

The research

How best to turn that context into judgment. A frontier model on its own is a general reasoner pointed at a specialist problem.

Language models
Where the work really is language — reading the unstructured record, forming hypotheses, pressure-testing a recommendation.
Statistical methods
Priors and shrinkage, hierarchical fitting across accounts, change-point detection, and the calibration that makes a stated confidence mean what it says. Language models are bad at arithmetic over long series.
Causal inference
Holdouts, incrementality and counterfactual baselines — separating what we caused from what was going to happen anyway.
Simulation
Modelling markets rather than individuals, and learning policies that hold across the range of response curves the evidence permits.
Purpose-trained models
Small models for the jobs where consistency beats intelligence, trained for the field rather than borrowed from everything at once.
Value of information
What is worth looking at before deciding, and when more evidence stops moving the answer. The formal version of the question the context layer currently answers by heuristic.

We think this is where the step change comes from, rather than from a larger general model.

Largely works today Being built The open problem
Execution largely works today. The context layer and retrieval over it are what we are building. Diagnosis, strategy and learning are the open problem — along with the harder half of selection: what to look at before you know what is wrong.

No two businesses are the same problem.

Which frameworks apply, what data exists, which methods identify anything at all — these change from one business to the next. Ship a single fixed model and you have described an average company that doesn’t exist.

So the structure is shared and the fit is not. The record, the machinery and the method library are common to every business. Which methods apply, how far each one is trusted, and where the thresholds sit are fitted per business and re-calibrated as evidence arrives — by the system, not by hand.

The end state is judgment across every decision a business makes.

Each function needs its own context and builds its own record — the machinery is the same, what it learns is not.

We start in marketing.

We run an agency, so we have the accounts, the operators and the decisions — and the standing to require experiments rather than ask for them. Marketing judgment is opinionated, constantly exercised, and it settles against numbers on a timescale you can learn from.

If the method holds here, it holds in quieter places.
